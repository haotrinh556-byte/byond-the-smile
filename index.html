The invisible illness / Depression awareness

Not every illness is visible.

A smile does not tell the whole story. Look beyond appearances, challenge the stigma surrounding depression, and learn how to support others.

Explore the experience ↓

01 / Understand

Depression is more than sadness.

Major depressive disorder is a mental health condition that can affect emotions, thinking, physical energy and daily functioning. It is not a character flaw or a lack of motivation.

01 — Mind

Thoughts and emotions

Persistent low mood, hopelessness, difficulty focusing or losing interest in activities.

02 — Body

Physical experiences

Changes in sleep or appetite, exhaustion and sometimes unexplained aches.

03 — Daily life

Everyday challenges

Studying, socializing and completing ordinary tasks can become difficult.

Experiences vary. Symptoms that persist and interfere with daily life deserve professional attention. Depression is treatable. Learn more from NIMH ↗

03 / Challenge stigma

Unlearn the myths.

Stigma can make people feel ashamed, isolated or reluctant to seek care. Select each statement to reveal the reality.

“Depression is just being sad.”

Depression is a health condition that can affect multiple areas of life, not simply a temporary emotion.

“People with depression are lazy.”

Low energy and difficulties concentrating may be symptoms. Blame adds stigma instead of addressing the problem.

“If someone smiles, they cannot be depressed.”

People experience and express depression differently. Outward behavior does not establish whether someone has depression.

“Asking for help is a sign of weakness.”

Seeking care is a meaningful step toward recovery. Treatment and support can help.

Public stigma

Negative stereotypes and prejudiced reactions from others.

Self-stigma

Internalizing negative beliefs about one's own condition.

Structural stigma

Policies or practices that disadvantage people with mental illness.

05 / Take action

Be part of the change.

You do not have to be a mental health professional to respond with compassion.

Listen

Ask how someone is doing. Give them space to speak without judgment.

Change your language

Replace “just get over it” with “I'm here for you.” Avoid treating a diagnosis as someone's identity.

Encourage support

Offer to help someone connect with a counselor or health professional, while respecting their choices.

Support is available.

Temple students can explore Tuttleman Counseling Services. In the U.S., call or text 988 for crisis support. Call 911 in an immediate life-threatening emergency.
